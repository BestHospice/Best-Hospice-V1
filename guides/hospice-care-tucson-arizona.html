Hospice Care in Tucson, Arizona

End-of-life care resources for Tucson, Green Valley, Oro Valley, Marana, and the broader southern Arizona region

Hospice Care in Tucson and Southern Arizona

Tucson is Arizona's second-largest city and the seat of Pima County, situated in the Sonoran Desert at an elevation of roughly 2,400 feet. The Tucson metropolitan statistical area includes the cities of South Tucson, Oro Valley, and Marana to the north; Sahuarita to the south; and the unincorporated community of Green Valley approximately 25 miles south of the city. The University of Arizona — one of the nation's flagship public research universities — anchors Tucson's identity as a regional academic, medical, and cultural center for all of southern Arizona. Davis-Monthan Air Force Base, located within Tucson's city limits, brings a substantial active-duty and veteran population to the area, contributing to high demand for VA-coordinated end-of-life care services alongside civilian hospice programs.

Tucson's retiree population is significant and has driven steady growth in demand for hospice and palliative care over the past two decades. The Green Valley retirement community, with approximately 20,000 residents concentrated in a planned area south of the city, represents one of the densest concentrations of older adults in all of Arizona outside the Phoenix metro's Sun Cities. Pima County's demographic landscape also includes large Latino and Native American communities — particularly the Tohono O'odham Nation to the west and southwest and the Pascua Yaqui Tribe within Tucson itself — whose end-of-life care traditions and preferences may require culturally responsive, specialized hospice services. The combination of a large university hospital, several community hospitals, a major retirement corridor, veteran communities, and diverse cultural populations makes Tucson one of the most complex and important hospice markets in the American Southwest.

Major Health Systems and Hospice Transitions in Tucson

Banner University Medical Center Tucson — formerly University Medical Center and affiliated with the University of Arizona — is the primary academic medical center for southern Arizona and the most common origin point for complex hospice transitions in the region. It is home to the University of Arizona Cancer Center, a National Cancer Institute (NCI)-designated Comprehensive Cancer Center, which provides oncology care to patients from throughout southern Arizona, northern Mexico, and beyond. Patients completing active cancer treatment at the UArizona Cancer Center who transition to comfort-focused care are often referred to Tucson-area hospice agencies through the Cancer Center's palliative care and social work teams. Banner University Medical Center Tucson is also a Level 1 trauma center and the primary referral destination for complex cases across a broad geographic catchment area.

Carondelet Health Network, now operating under CommonSpirit Health, runs two community hospitals in Tucson: St. Mary's Hospital on the west side of the city and St. Joseph's Hospital on the east side. Both facilities provide community-level acute care and have palliative care coordination resources. Tucson Medical Center (TMC) is a large independent nonprofit community hospital serving central and east Tucson with a broad range of services including a palliative care program that helps patients and families plan for end-of-life care. Northwest Medical Center, affiliated with Steward Health Care, serves the fast-growing northwest Tucson and Marana communities. As residential growth has extended north along the I-10 corridor into Marana and Oro Valley, Northwest Medical Center has become an increasingly important point of hospice entry for residents in those communities.

For veterans in the Tucson area, the Southern Arizona VA Health Care System — headquartered in Tucson — provides a full range of services including palliative care and hospice coordination. Veterans who qualify may receive hospice care through the VA, through Medicare, or through a combination of both programs. Some Tucson-area community hospice agencies have formalized relationships with the Southern Arizona VA to provide community-based hospice services to veterans who prefer to remain at home rather than use VA inpatient resources.

Medicare and Arizona Medicaid Coverage in Tucson

Medicare covers hospice services for beneficiaries with a terminal prognosis of six months or less if the disease runs its expected course, and who choose to stop curative treatment in favor of comfort-focused care. The Medicare Hospice Benefit covers an interdisciplinary team (physician, nurse, social worker, chaplain, home health aide, and volunteer), all medications related to the terminal diagnosis, durable medical equipment, and medical supplies — with no copayments or deductibles for the covered services. For Tucson-area patients enrolled in Medicare Advantage plans through carriers such as UnitedHealthcare, Humana, Aetna, or Banner University Health Plans, those plans are required to provide the same hospice benefit as Original Medicare, and may offer additional supplemental benefits. Older adults in Green Valley and Sahuarita who are enrolled in Medicare Advantage should verify that their plan's network includes hospice agencies serving those communities south of Tucson.

For Tucson-area residents who are low-income and qualify for both Medicare and Arizona Medicaid, the relevant program is ALTCS (Arizona Long-Term Care System), administered by AHCCCS. Enrollment in ALTCS is through AHCCCS managed care plans, which in southern Arizona include Aetna Better Health of Arizona, Care1st Health Plan Arizona, Molina Healthcare of Arizona, and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan of Arizona. When a dual-eligible patient elects the Medicare Hospice Benefit, Medicare is the primary payer for the hospice interdisciplinary team and related services. ALTCS may continue to cover room and board costs for patients residing in a Medicaid-certified nursing facility — a benefit that is separate from but complementary to the Medicare Hospice Benefit. Tucson-area families can access free, unbiased guidance on Medicare and ALTCS from Arizona's SHIP (State Health Insurance Assistance Program) counselors, administered through the Arizona Department of Economic Security's Division of Aging and Adult Services. SHIP counselors can walk families through benefit coordination questions in plain language and at no cost.

Arizona Hospice Laws, Licensing & Benefits

Hospice agencies operating in Tucson and throughout Arizona must hold a license from the Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS), Division of Licensing Services. Agencies seeking Medicare reimbursement must also be certified by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) under the federal Conditions of Participation for hospice. In practice, federal Medicare certification and ADHS state licensure are closely aligned — meeting federal standards largely satisfies state licensing requirements, and surveyors from both agencies look at similar dimensions of care quality, documentation, and patient rights. Families can verify that a Tucson hospice agency holds a current ADHS license through the ADHS online licensing search tool.

Arizona does not require a Certificate of Need (CON) for hospice licensure. Unlike states with CON laws — which require new hospice providers to demonstrate to the state that market demand supports a new program before they can open — Arizona allows any provider meeting licensing and certification standards to operate. The result is a competitive hospice market in Tucson and Pima County, with options ranging from locally founded agencies that have served the community for decades to regional and national organizations that have expanded into the southern Arizona market.

Arizona law recognizes three key advance directive instruments that hospice teams work with routinely. The Pre-Hospital Medical Care Directive — universally known as the "orange form" because state law requires it to be printed on bright orange paper — is a physician-signed document instructing emergency responders not to attempt resuscitation outside a hospital. Having a correctly completed and visibly posted orange form in the home is critical; without it, 911 responders are generally obligated to begin resuscitation. The Arizona Healthcare Power of Attorney is a legal document designating an agent to make healthcare decisions if the patient loses decision-making capacity. Arizona also uses the POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment), a portable physician order that specifies preferences for CPR, mechanical ventilation, and medically administered nutrition, and that follows the patient across care settings — from home to emergency department to skilled nursing facility. Tucson hospice intake nurses are required to review advance directives at the time of admission and will assist families in completing, updating, or locating any of these documents.

How to Choose a Hospice Provider in Tucson

  • Confirm coverage of your specific location. Tucson hospice service areas vary. If your loved one is in Green Valley, Sahuarita, Marana, Oro Valley, or a rural area outside the city, confirm explicitly that the agency routinely staffs your area and ask about their typical after-hours response time to your location.
  • Ask about veteran-specific experience. If your loved one is a veteran, ask whether the agency has a We Honor Veterans designation or formal partnership with the Southern Arizona VA Health Care System. Ask whether a VA benefit coordinator has worked with the agency before.
  • Inquire about cultural and language competency. Tucson's significant Latino, Tohono O'odham, and Pascua Yaqui communities have distinct end-of-life traditions. If your family speaks Spanish or belongs to a Native American community, ask specifically about the agency's clinical staff language ability and experience with your community.
  • Understand coverage south into Green Valley. Green Valley's large retirement community has no local acute-care hospital. Ask how the agency manages after-hours crisis visits in Green Valley and whether nurses are familiar with the area's medical facilities and care managers.
  • Evaluate the inpatient hospice option. For symptom crises that cannot be managed at home, ask where inpatient hospice care would be provided — and whether that facility is within a reasonable distance for your family to visit.
  • Ask about oncology-specific experience. Given the University of Arizona Cancer Center's regional reach, many Tucson hospice patients are transitioning from active cancer treatment. Ask how the agency communicates with oncology teams and how they manage complex cancer-related symptoms.
  • Verify ADHS licensure and Medicare certification. Both can be confirmed independently before you commit to a provider.
  • Ask about bereavement services. Medicare requires hospice agencies to provide bereavement follow-up to the family for at least 13 months after death. Ask what the program looks like and whether it is delivered by the agency's own staff.

Frequently Asked Questions — Hospice Care in Tucson

Do Tucson hospice agencies serve Green Valley, which is 25 miles south of the city?

Yes. Most hospice agencies based in Tucson explicitly include Green Valley and Sahuarita in their service areas, and many are experienced in serving the large retiree community concentrated in Green Valley's planned neighborhoods. Green Valley has its own medical facilities but lacks a full acute-care hospital, so hospice agencies providing after-hours nurse visits must maintain reliable staffing coverage for the drive south of Tucson. When evaluating agencies, confirm specifically that Green Valley is within their standard coverage zone and ask how they staff overnight and weekend response in that corridor.

Does Arizona require a Certificate of Need (CON) to open a hospice in Tucson?

No. Arizona does not require a Certificate of Need for hospice licensure. Any provider meeting Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS) licensing standards and Medicare certification requirements may operate in the state. Tucson families therefore have access to a range of choices including locally owned agencies, regional hospice organizations, and national providers — and that competitive environment continues to grow as Pima County's senior population expands.

How does ALTCS work for hospice patients in the Tucson area?

ALTCS (Arizona Long-Term Care System) is Arizona's Medicaid long-term care program, administered by AHCCCS. For dual-eligible Tucson-area patients — those who qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid — ALTCS managed care plans including Aetna Better Health of Arizona, Care1st Health Plan Arizona, Molina Healthcare of Arizona, and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan of Arizona coordinate benefits. When a dual-eligible patient elects the Medicare Hospice Benefit, Medicare is the primary payer for hospice services. ALTCS may cover room and board costs for patients living in a Medicaid-certified nursing facility. Arizona's free SHIP counselors can help Tucson families understand how their specific combination of Medicare and ALTCS benefits applies to hospice care.

Are there hospice providers in Tucson with experience serving Native American patients from the Tohono O'odham Nation or Pascua Yaqui Tribe?

Some Tucson-area hospice agencies have developed relationships and cultural competency protocols for serving patients from the Tohono O'odham Nation and the Pascua Yaqui Tribe. End-of-life care preferences, spiritual practices, and family decision-making structures may differ significantly from mainstream norms, and culturally responsive care requires intentional preparation by the agency. When evaluating agencies for a Native American patient, ask directly about the agency's experience, relationships with tribal health programs, and whether they have staff with relevant cultural background or training. The Indian Health Service and tribal health programs may also have their own hospice resources or referral relationships.

What advance directive documents does Arizona use and will the hospice team help with them?

Arizona uses three key advance directive tools. The Pre-Hospital Medical Care Directive — known informally as the "orange form" — is a physician-signed document instructing emergency responders not to attempt resuscitation outside a hospital. It must be printed on orange paper and posted visibly in the home. The Arizona Healthcare Power of Attorney designates an agent to make medical decisions on behalf of a patient who cannot speak for themselves. The POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) is a physician order that travels with the patient across care settings and specifies preferences for CPR, mechanical ventilation, and artificial nutrition. Tucson hospice intake teams are required to discuss advance directives at admission and will help families complete, update, or locate signed copies of any of these documents.
