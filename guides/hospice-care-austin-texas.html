Finding hospice care in Austin

Austin is the capital of Texas and the fourth-largest city in the United States, with over 960,000 residents in the city proper and more than 2.3 million across the Austin-Round Rock-Georgetown metropolitan statistical area. Austin is one of the fastest-growing major cities in the country, driven by technology industry expansion — Apple, Tesla, Dell, Oracle, Samsung, and dozens of high-growth startups have major Austin presences — as well as the University of Texas at Austin and sustained domestic migration from other states.

Austin's historically young demographic is a defining characteristic of its hospice market. Compared to older metros like Houston or Dallas, Austin's provider infrastructure is smaller relative to population — a consequence of decades of in-migration skewing young. As that population ages and the broader metro absorbs retirees relocating from other parts of the country, demand for hospice and palliative care services is growing faster than provider capacity in some parts of the metro. Families searching for hospice in Austin often begin online — a reflection of the tech-fluent, research-oriented culture of the city.

Austin's diversity is also growing. Large Hispanic/Latino, South Asian, East Asian, and East African communities have developed through technology and university employment, making multilingual hospice capacity increasingly relevant. If language accessibility matters for your family, ask prospective providers directly about bilingual staff and interpreter services.

Key questions to ask Austin hospice providers before enrolling:

  • What is your after-hours nursing response time — how quickly does a nurse arrive?
  • What is your current nurse-to-patient ratio?
  • Do you cover my specific ZIP code — including Round Rock, Georgetown, Cedar Park, Kyle, or Buda?
  • Do you accept Medicare, STAR+PLUS Medicaid, and/or Medicare Advantage?
  • Do you offer bilingual services in Spanish or other languages?
  • What is your experience with my loved one's specific diagnosis?
  • Are you licensed by Texas HHSC and Medicare-certified?

Austin hospice coverage area

The Austin metro spans five counties, and hospice providers based in central Austin vary significantly in how far they extend into the suburbs. The rapid growth of Williamson and Hays counties means many patients now live in communities that were rural a decade ago. Always confirm coverage explicitly for your specific ZIP code before enrolling. Common coverage communities in the Austin metro include:

  • Austin (all ZIP codes — Downtown, South Austin, East Austin, North Austin, Northwest Hills, Westlake)
  • Round Rock, Georgetown, Leander, Cedar Park, Pflugerville (Williamson County)
  • Kyle, Buda (Hays County)
  • San Marcos (Hays County — confirm coverage; some providers do not extend this far south)
  • Bastrop, Elgin (Bastrop County — confirm coverage)
  • Lockhart (Caldwell County — confirm coverage)
  • Marble Falls, Burnet (Burnet County — confirm coverage)

Providers based in central Travis County may not reliably cover Hays, Caldwell, or Bastrop County addresses. If your family member lives in the southern or eastern suburbs, confirm the provider's specific service boundary rather than assuming it follows county lines.

Austin health systems and hospice transitions

Austin's hospital landscape is organized around two dominant systems — Ascension Seton and St. David's HealthCare — with newer entrants from Baylor Scott & White and UT Health Austin expanding access. Case managers, social workers, and palliative care teams at these systems coordinate hospice transitions when curative treatment is no longer the appropriate goal. Key Austin-area health systems include:

  • Ascension Seton (formerly Seton Healthcare Family) — The largest hospital system in the Austin area, operating Ascension Seton Medical Center Austin, Dell Seton Medical Center at The University of Texas (the region's Level I trauma center and a teaching hospital in partnership with Dell Medical School), Ascension Seton Medical Center Williamson in Round Rock, Ascension Seton Northwest, and Ascension Seton Hays in Kyle. Ascension Seton's palliative care program coordinates hospice transitions across this network; patients at any campus should ask to speak with a palliative care team member or social worker when hospice is being considered. The Hays campus in Kyle extends Ascension Seton's reach into the fast-growing southern corridor.
  • St. David's HealthCare (HCA Healthcare) — Austin's other major hospital system, operating St. David's Medical Center (flagship, central Austin), St. David's North Austin Medical Center, St. David's Round Rock Medical Center, St. David's Georgetown Hospital, and St. David's South Austin Medical Center. St. David's operates a large cancer center and has a close affiliation with Texas Oncology, making it a frequent referral source for hospice among cancer patients. Case managers across St. David's campuses coordinate community hospice transitions; families should ask to speak with a social worker or case manager when the hospice conversation is appropriate.
  • Baylor Scott & White Health — Has an expanding presence in the Austin area through Baylor Scott & White Medical Center – Marble Falls and outpatient facilities in the region. Patients in the Marble Falls and Burnet County area receiving Baylor Scott & White care should ask about hospice coordination through that system's case management team.
  • University of Texas Health Austin (Dell Medical School) — A newer academic health system embedded at the University of Texas campus, building clinical programs including palliative care. Dell Medical School's clinical programs are still developing, but the institution is establishing palliative care training and services as part of its academic mission. Patients receiving care through UT Health Austin should ask whether palliative care consultations are available.

If the hospital team has not initiated a hospice or palliative care conversation and you believe it may be appropriate, ask directly: "Can we speak with a social worker or palliative care team about whether hospice is right for our family?" You may also contact an Austin hospice provider directly — providers can assess eligibility independently, without going through a hospital referral.

Medicare and Texas Medicaid coverage in Austin

Medicare Part A covers hospice comprehensively for eligible Austin patients in 2026:

  • Nursing visits — $0
  • Comfort medications related to the terminal diagnosis — $0 or up to $5 per prescription
  • Medical equipment (hospital bed, oxygen concentrator, wheelchair, bedside commode) — $0
  • Home health aide and homemaker services — $0
  • Social worker, chaplain, and bereavement counseling — $0
  • Inpatient respite care — 5% coinsurance

Texas STAR+PLUS Medicaid covers hospice for qualifying low-income Austin-area residents. STAR+PLUS is Texas's Medicaid managed care program for adults with disabilities and elderly Texans, providing home and community-based services alongside managed acute care. Managed care organizations serving Travis, Williamson, and Hays counties include Aetna Better Health of Texas, Humana Healthy Horizons in Texas, Molina Healthcare of Texas, Superior HealthPlan (Centene), and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan of Texas. Availability varies by plan and county — confirm which STAR+PLUS plan is active and how it interacts with the hospice benefit before enrollment.

Texas also offers the Community Based Alternatives (CBA) waiver, which provides home- and community-based services as an alternative to nursing facility placement for dual-eligible and Medicaid-eligible individuals. Families whose loved one is already receiving CBA waiver services should ask the hospice provider how hospice enrollment affects existing Medicaid waiver benefits.

Medicare Advantage plans are broadly available in the Austin market — major carriers include UnitedHealthcare, Humana, Aetna, and Blue Cross Blue Shield of Texas. Medicare Advantage plans must provide hospice coverage at least equivalent to Medicare Part A. However, some Medicare Advantage plans use a carve-out arrangement where hospice is paid under traditional Medicare Part A rather than through the Advantage plan — confirm this detail with the provider before enrolling.

Texas's SHIP program — administered through the Texas Health and Human Services Commission — provides free Medicare counseling through trained volunteer counselors across Texas. SHIP counselors can help Austin-area families understand how STAR+PLUS interacts with hospice, evaluate Medicare Advantage plan hospice provisions, and navigate coverage questions. Contact Texas HHSC to locate a SHIP counselor in the Austin area.

Texas laws and regulations affecting hospice in Austin

No Certificate of Need requirement: Texas does not require a Certificate of Need (CON) for hospice licensure. Any provider meeting Texas state licensing requirements and achieving Medicare certification may operate in Austin. This open-market structure contributes to the range of hospice options available across the metro, but also means families must do due diligence — verify that any provider is currently licensed by Texas HHSC and holds Medicare certification before enrolling.

Hospice licensing authority: Texas licenses hospice providers through the Texas Health and Human Services Commission (HHSC). All Medicare-certified Texas hospice providers meet HHSC licensing requirements as a condition of federal certification. Families can verify a provider's current license status through HHSC's online provider lookup.

Advance directive forms in Texas: Texas uses specific legal documents to record end-of-life care preferences. These forms differ from those used in other states, and families relocating to Austin from other states may need to execute new Texas-specific documents. The key Texas forms are:

  • Directive to Physicians, Family, or Surrogates (Texas Living Will) — A written statement of the patient's wishes regarding life-sustaining treatment in the event of a terminal or irreversible condition. Recognized under the Texas Advance Directives Act, this document guides treatment decisions when the patient cannot communicate. It is not a physician order — it is a statement of patient preferences that informs physician and family decision-making.
  • Medical Power of Attorney — Designates an agent (typically a family member) to make medical decisions on behalf of the patient when the patient is incapacitated. In Texas, the Medical Power of Attorney becomes active when the patient's physician determines the patient lacks decision-making capacity. This is the primary legal tool families use to make hospice enrollment decisions when a patient cannot do so themselves.
  • Out-of-Hospital Do-Not-Resuscitate Order (OOH-DNR) — Texas does not use the POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) form used in many other states. Instead, Texas has the OOH-DNR: a physician-signed portable medical order directing EMS and other emergency responders not to attempt resuscitation outside a hospital setting. This document is critical for hospice patients: without a signed OOH-DNR in the home, Texas law requires EMS to attempt resuscitation even if the patient and family have expressed a clear wish to avoid it. Austin hospice providers routinely help families complete or update the OOH-DNR at the time of enrollment — if this has not happened by the end of the intake visit, ask the nurse about it directly.

Families moving to Austin from other states — a particularly common scenario given Austin's high domestic in-migration — should note that advance directives executed in other states may not be recognized in the same form under Texas law. Ask your Austin hospice provider's social worker to review any existing documents and advise whether new Texas-specific forms should be executed.

How to choose a hospice provider in Austin

Austin's fast-growing, tech-oriented population means families searching for hospice often begin with online research and expect transparent information. Here is a practical framework for evaluating Austin hospice providers:

  • Verify Texas HHSC licensure and Medicare certification. This is the baseline. Confirm the provider holds a current Texas HHSC hospice license and is Medicare-certified. Both verifications can be done through HHSC's online portal and Medicare's Care Compare tool at medicare.gov.
  • Ask about after-hours nursing response. The quality of care during a nighttime or weekend crisis is the most important test of a hospice provider. Ask specifically: how quickly does a nurse arrive at the bedside — not just how quickly they call back — when a patient is in distress at 2 a.m. on a Sunday? Get a specific time commitment.
  • Understand geographic coverage precisely. Austin's sprawl and rapid suburban expansion mean a provider based in central Austin may not reach Round Rock, Cedar Park, Kyle, or Buda effectively. Do not assume coverage extends to your loved one's address — confirm the specific ZIP code is in the provider's service area.
  • Ask about nurse-to-patient ratios. Austin's hospice market has tighter staffing relative to population than Houston or Dallas. A provider managing rapid growth may be operating with stretched nursing teams. Ask directly how many patients each nurse currently carries and whether staffing has kept pace with enrollment growth.
  • Assess multilingual capacity. Austin's growing Hispanic/Latino, South Asian, East Asian, and East African communities reflect the city's tech and university workforce. If your family needs hospice communications in Spanish, Telugu, Tamil, Mandarin, Amharic, or another language, ask whether bilingual staff — not just a telephone interpreter service — are available.
  • Evaluate inpatient care access. Medicare's hospice benefit includes inpatient respite care (up to five consecutive days in a facility) and continuous home nursing during a medical crisis. Ask the provider where they place patients who need inpatient care and whether that facility is accessible from your loved one's location.
  • Confirm insurance acceptance before the intake visit. Providers accepting Medicare are obligated to accept the Medicare hospice rate. For STAR+PLUS, Medicare Advantage, or employer insurance, confirm acceptance before the intake visit rather than discovering a coverage problem after enrollment begins.

Does Medicare cover hospice in Austin?

Yes. Medicare Part A covers nursing visits, comfort medications, medical equipment, home health aide, social work, chaplain services, and bereavement counseling at little to no cost. A physician must certify a prognosis of six months or less. Ascension Seton and St. David's HealthCare physicians are experienced with the certification process.

Does Texas STAR+PLUS Medicaid cover hospice in Austin?

Yes. STAR+PLUS covers hospice for qualifying low-income Austin-area residents. Managed care organizations serving Travis, Williamson, and Hays counties include Aetna Better Health of Texas, Humana Healthy Horizons, Molina Healthcare, Superior HealthPlan, and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan. Confirm plan availability and interaction with the hospice benefit before enrolling.

Does Texas use POLST forms for hospice patients?

No. Texas does not use POLST. Texas uses the Out-of-Hospital Do-Not-Resuscitate Order (OOH-DNR) as its portable medical order for patients who do not want resuscitation outside a hospital. Texas also uses the Directive to Physicians, Family, or Surrogates (Texas Living Will) and the Medical Power of Attorney. Austin hospice providers help families complete these Texas-specific documents at enrollment — families relocating from other states should have their existing advance directives reviewed for Texas validity.

How quickly can hospice start in Austin?

Most Austin hospice providers can begin within 24 to 48 hours of initial contact. Same-day starts are possible for urgent hospital discharge situations from Ascension Seton or St. David's campuses. The provider handles physician certification and insurance verification before or during the first visit. Confirm startup timelines when you call — response capacity varies by provider.
